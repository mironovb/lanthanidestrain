Dear Dr. Vogiatzis,

Thank you for the notes. The representation comparison you pointed me to is
done, and here are the results I obtained:

The quantity in the figure is not overall log D. It is R^2 for the predicted
difference in log D between two adjacent lanthanides, measured with the same
extractant under the same conditions. Industrially, that is the separation that
matters, and it is also the hardest case, since neighbouring ionic radii differ
by only about 0.013 angstroms. Zero on that axis means the model does no better
than assuming every adjacent pair separates by the average amount.
Cross-validation is leave-extractants-out, five folds repeated three times, so a
ligand never sits in both training and test. The dataset covers 4,746
measurements over 162 extractants and 14 lanthanides.

Since the 3D encoder is the only unfamiliar piece, here is what it is. Each
complex, meaning the metal with its ligands and the inner-sphere nitrate or
water, is relaxed with GFN2-xTB. From the relaxed geometry I build a
Vietoris-Rips complex at 3.5 angstroms: atoms become nodes, atom pairs closer
than that radius become edges, and mutually close triples become triangles. A
network then passes messages along that object for three rounds, nodes exchanging
information through their edges and edges through their triangles, and returns
one vector per complex. There are 953 distinct complexes behind the 4,746 rows,
since rows sharing a structure differ only in conditions.

No coordinate enters the network. The node inputs are the xTB partial charge
with a flag marking where that charge is missing, whether the atom is the metal
or a donor, and the distance to the metal, while edges and triangles carry the
radius at which they appear. Invariance to rotation, translation and reflection
is therefore structural rather than learned or trained in by augmentation.
Pooling is metal-centred, a soft histogram of distance to the metal, which I
added after measuring that ordinary mean pooling over roughly a hundred atoms
averages away exactly the sub-0.1 angstrom shifts of the coordination shell that
the contraction lives in. Without that term the encoder recovered the lanthanide
index at R^2 = 0.016, while eight hand-made metal-ligand distance summaries
reached 0.57 on the same folds. The pooled vector is then concatenated with the
experimental condition columns and passed to a regression head for log D. It is
trained with a term on the within-block adjacent difference rather than on the
absolute value alone, and checkpoints are selected on the adjacent-pair metric
rather than on overall error, since those turn out to be different objectives.
The number in the figure is a 16-seed ensemble scored out of fold.

Three groups of bars make up the figure. The top three are single models:
gradient boosting on 2D descriptors reaches 0.142, the fingerprint network 0.221,
and a neural encoder over the 3D complex 0.238. In the middle sits that 3D
encoder combined with the fingerprint network, at 0.251. The bottom three show
the three-model combination we would actually deploy. It scores 0.226 with no 3D
input, 0.221 when the third slot holds a matched model without 3D input, and
0.267 with the 3D encoder in that slot.

That control is why I read the result as structure rather than an ensembling
artefact. A second model often helps a combination simply because its errors
differ, so I built one with the same architecture, objective, folds, and seeds,
removed only the 3D encoder, and put it in the same slot. Every contrast and its
decision rule was committed before the numbers existed. The bootstrap resamples
whole extractants, since rows on one ligand are not independent.

However, the effect is not really topological. I built the complexes as
Vietoris-Rips filtrations, yet deleting the triangles costs nothing, and a plain
distance network with no simplices earns the same slot. What helps is a learned
3D representation, not the complex itself.

Put simply, the fingerprint models see the ligand as a drawing of its bonds, and
that drawing is the same picture no matter which lanthanide sits in it. Those
models can only tell La from Ce through the conditions and through whatever the
tabulated ionic radius carries. The 3D encoder sees the assembled complex
instead, where the donor atoms sit at slightly different distances around each
metal, so La and Ce arrive as two different pictures rather than one. The
difference between those pictures is tiny, on the order of a hundredth of an
angstrom in the coordination shell, which is why the model is built to keep
shell distances instead of averaging them into a single number. The result is
that the pictures carry real information about which of two neighbours is
extracted better, information the drawing does not have, and it is the
three-dimensional arrangement doing that work rather than anything topological.
