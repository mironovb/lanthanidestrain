\begin{abstract}
Separating neighbouring lanthanides is the hard step in rare-earth processing, and whether a candidate extractant will do it is not predictable from first principles.
We learn it from data: 4{,}746 curated solvent-extraction measurements on 162 extractants, scored on the separation factors of adjacent lanthanide pairs under identical conditions, with extractants held out.
A gradient-boosting model that predicts the overall extraction level and the metal-to-metal differences of a series with two separate models reaches $R^2 = +0.32$ on 905 held-out adjacent pairs, up from $+0.27$ for the same learner trained on the raw target, and a 3D graph network on the metal--ligand complexes adds a small, pre-registered gain to $+0.33$ ($r = 0.59$).
Ranking held-out extractants for a given separation, the model's top quartile is genuinely top-quartile half of the time (chance 0.25), and it detects separations with $\mathrm{SF} \geq 2$ with AUC 0.81.
We also show why 3D structure contributes so little: across 71 ligands and 15 lanthanides, GFN2-xTB geometries reproduce only 40\,\% of the lanthanide contraction, because its lanthanide parameters are linear in $Z$, so a complex geometry carries one number about the metal; g-xTB (f electrons in the valence) reproduces the contraction within 8\,\%, and the new Ln-xTB parameters recover the mean slope but not a smooth series.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The trivalent lanthanides differ from their neighbours by a few hundredths of an \AA{}ngstr\"om in ionic radius and by almost nothing else that a ligand can sense, which is why their separation by solvent extraction takes dozens of stages and why the search for more selective extractants is still largely empirical~\cite{cheisson2019,nash1993}.
The practical question is narrow: given a candidate ligand and a set of extraction conditions, how well will it separate a specific adjacent pair, say Nd from Pr or Dy from Tb?
The separation factor $\mathrm{SF} = D_1/D_2$ is a ratio of two distribution ratios measured under the same conditions, so most of what makes $D$ hard to predict (diluent, acid, concentrations, analytical scatter) cancels; what remains is the ligand's discrimination between two nearly identical ions.

This paper reports what can be learned about that discrimination from the literature record, with a strict held-out-extractant protocol, and what cannot.
The contributions are:
(i) a scoring construction that isolates adjacent-pair selectivity from the extraction level (Section~\ref{sec:task});
(ii) the observation that predicting the level of a series and its shape with two separate models is worth more than any representation change we tried, $+0.27 \to +0.32$ with the same learner and features (Section~\ref{sec:models});
(iii) a 3D graph network whose within-series predictions add a small gain that survives a pre-registered test on pairs never used for any decision, the only configuration in which 3D helped (Section~\ref{sec:results});
(iv) a decision-quality read-out (ranking, enrichment, calibrated intervals) that says what a screening user actually gets from $R^2 = 0.33$; and
(v) a benchmark of three semiempirical Hamiltonians on 71 lanthanide complexes that explains, mechanistically, why the geometries carry so little information about the metal (Section~\ref{sec:chem}).
Negative results are reported alongside, because for this problem they are most of the picture.

\section{Data and task}
\label{sec:task}

\paragraph{Data.}
Measurements come from the SAFE solvent-extraction database~\cite{safe} (48{,}138 rows, 31 elements, 181 source publications).
We keep lanthanide(III) rows with a resolvable extractant structure, canonicalise the extractant SMILES, deduplicate exact replicates, and drop rows whose distribution ratio is a detection-limit placeholder.
This leaves 5{,}992 measurements of $\log D$ on 190 extractants; 4{,}746 of them (162 extractants) have a 3D complex and form the modelling population, so that every model is compared on the same rows.
Each row carries the extractant, the metal, and 64 condition columns (acid and its concentration, extractant concentration, diluent, modifier, temperature, phase ratio, contact time, metal concentration).

\paragraph{Target.}
A separation factor is only defined between measurements made under the same conditions.
We therefore group rows into \emph{blocks}: one extractant (by canonical SMILES) and one bin of each of ten condition descriptors.
Within a block, replicate measurements of the same metal are averaged into a cell, and every pair of cells whose lanthanides are neighbours in the series ($|\Delta Z| = 1$; promethium is absent, so Nd--Sm is not adjacent) yields one target value
\[
	y = \log D_{\mathrm{Ln}_i} - \log D_{\mathrm{Ln}_{i+1}} = \log \mathrm{SF}_{i,i+1}.
\]
Models are trained on $\log D$ at the row level and evaluated after the same block, cell and pair construction is applied to their out-of-fold predictions.
The score is the coefficient of determination $R^2$ of predicted against measured $\log \mathrm{SF}$ over all adjacent pairs; a model that predicts every $\log D$ perfectly up to a block-wise constant scores 1, and one that only knows the extraction level scores 0.
The modelling population yields 905 adjacent pairs on 76 extractants.
A second, expanded population (rows without curated geometries, 1{,}220 pairs) is used for confirmation only.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
All predictions are out-of-fold under 5-fold cross-validation grouped by extractant, repeated three times; an extractant's rows never appear in both training and test.
Because the score is a single number over 905 correlated pairs, we report a cluster bootstrap over extractants and paired seed contrasts for model comparisons.
One set of 444 adjacent pairs, formed from rows outside the modelling population, was frozen before any 3D model was built and used exactly once, for the pre-registered confirmation in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Models}
\label{sec:models}

\begin{figure}[t]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{architecture}
	\caption{The system. A level model predicts $\log D$ and is used only through its block mean (the anchor). A shape model predicts $\log D$ minus the block mean. A 3D graph network on the GFN2-xTB complex geometries contributes its own block-centred prediction. The final prediction is the anchor plus a weighted sum of the two shapes.}
	\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Features.}
Ligand descriptors from RDKit~\cite{rdkit} and 2048-bit Morgan fingerprints~\cite{rogers2010}; the metal's atomic number, position in the series and Shannon CN-8 radius~\cite{shannon1976}; and the 64 condition columns; 746 columns in total.

\paragraph{Level and shape.}
The learner is CatBoost~\cite{catboost2018} with a quantile loss ($\alpha = 0.6$), depth 9 and feature subsampling 0.3, selected on the adjacent-pair score.
Trained directly on $\log D$ it reaches $R^2 = +0.268$ on adjacent pairs (our July baseline).
The gain comes from separating two questions that the raw target mixes.
Let $\bar y_b$ be the mean $\log D$ of block $b$ in the training rows.
A \emph{level} model $f_L$ is trained on $\log D$ and used only through its block mean over the test block, $a_b = \mathrm{mean}_{i \in b} f_L(x_i)$; a \emph{shape} model $f_S$ is trained on the block-centred target $\log D_i - \bar y_{b(i)}$ and used centred within the test block.
The prediction is
\begin{equation}
	\hat y_i = a_{b(i)} + \big(f_S(x_i) - \mathrm{mean}_{j \in b(i)} f_S(x_j)\big).
	\label{eq:anchored}
\end{equation}
Nothing else changes: same learner, same features, same folds.
The shape model spends all of its capacity on the differences between metals, which is the only part the score sees, and it can no longer trade shape accuracy for level accuracy.
Over eight seeds this gives $R^2 = +0.318$ (Table~\ref{tab:main}).

\paragraph{3D channel.}
Complexes are built with Architector~\cite{taylor2023architector} (the extractant with nitrate or water completing the coordination sphere of Ln$^{3+}$, coordination numbers 8--10) and optimised with GFN2-xTB~\cite{bannwarth2019gfn2}; 956 complexes cover the modelling population.
The encoder is a continuous-filter convolutional network in the SchNet family~\cite{schutt2017schnet}: heavy atoms are nodes, atoms within 4\,\AA{} are connected, distances are expanded in 64 radial basis functions, three message-passing layers of width 96, and a readout that pools node and edge embeddings together with a metal-centred term and a radial shell histogram around the metal, because mean pooling alone averages away the sub-0.1\,\AA{} shifts the contraction lives in.
The pooled embedding is concatenated with the tabular features and read out by a two-layer head.
Training minimises a Huber loss on $\log D$ plus a pair-contrast term on all within-block pairs of the batch (weight 4, adjacent pairs weighted $3\times$) so that the network is trained on differences as well as levels.
Standalone, a 32-seed ensemble scores $+0.266$, no better than the boosting baseline.
Used through Eq.~\eqref{eq:anchored} as a second shape source with weight $w$,
\[
	\hat y_i = a_{b(i)} + (1-w)\, s^{\mathrm{tab}}_i + w\, s^{\mathrm{3D}}_i ,
\]
with $s$ the block-centred predictions, $w = 0.35$ chosen by a nested criterion over the 905 pairs, the score rises to $+0.326$.
A simplicial (Vietoris--Rips) variant with triangle message passing makes the same predictions as the distance graph (pair-level $r = 0.963$) and receives blend weight 0.01 when both are offered, so we report the distance graph.

\paragraph{The 3D gain is small and real.}
The blend improves on the level/shape model by $+0.008$ on the 905 pairs.
That is below the seed-to-seed spread of a single encoder run (sd 0.028), so it was tested where no decision had been made: on the 444 frozen pairs, with $w$ carried over unchanged, the blend beats the level/shape model by $+0.016$.
This is the only configuration in the project in which 3D information survived next to the strongest tabular model instead of being absorbed by it: the encoder enters through the shape channel, where the level model cannot compete with it.

\paragraph{Is $R^2 = 0.33$ a correlation?}
Predicted and measured $\log \mathrm{SF}$ correlate at $r = 0.59$ on held-out extractants.
A cluster bootstrap over extractants gives a 95\,\% interval of $[+0.21, +0.38]$ for $R^2$, and shuffling the predictions within each pair position, which preserves the shared series shape, gives $R^2 = -0.05$ (97.5th percentile $+0.003$).
Position means explain 10\,\% of the measured variance and extractant means 13\,\%; after removing both, the model still scores $R^2 = +0.23$ ($r = 0.49$) on the pure ligand-by-position interaction, which is the ligand-specific selectivity itself.
No single extractant changes the score by more than 0.06 when dropped.
The predictions are calibrated in the sense that the regression of truth on prediction has slope 1.3; their compressed range (sd 0.12 against 0.27 for the truth) is what a calibrated predictor at $r = 0.59$ looks like, not a defect, and isotonic recalibration does not raise the score.

\paragraph{What a screening user gets.}
$R^2$ is dominated by the tail: the top 10\,\% of pairs by $|\log \mathrm{SF}|$ carry 71\,\% of the variance, and on the 80\,\% of pairs with $|\log \mathrm{SF}| < 0.3$ the score is only $+0.12$, inside the measurement noise.
The practically relevant questions are answered directly (Figure~\ref{fig:ranking}).
Ranking held-out extractants for a given adjacent pair gives a mean Spearman correlation of 0.46 over the twelve positions; the predicted top quartile contains a measured top-quartile extractant 50\,\% of the time against 25\,\% by chance; the sign of the separation is right for 80\,\% of pairs with $|\log \mathrm{SF}| > 0.1$.
As a detector of large separations ($\mathrm{SF} \geq 2$, base rate 0.20) the same predictions give AUC 0.81, and the top 20\,\% by predicted magnitude are large separations 56\,\% of the time with the correct sign in 93\,\% of those.
Split-conformal intervals~\cite{vovk2005} on the pair residuals, calibrated leave-one-extractant-out, cover 79\,\% and 90\,\% at nominal 80\,\% and 90\,\%, with median half-widths of 0.22 and 0.32 log units.

\paragraph{What did not help.}
Table~\ref{tab:neg} lists the interventions that were tried under the same protocol.
Training on all within-block pairs rather than adjacent ones is already part of the contrast loss; a standalone model of pair differences reaches $+0.19$ but adds nothing to the row-level models.
Up-weighting the three metal-identity features in the shape model is neutral at $2\times$ and harmful beyond.
Persistence-homology descriptors of the complexes damage the shape model, and a block-mean control attributes 78\,\% of the damage to their within-block variation, i.e.\ to conformer noise.
Re-optimising the complexes with g-xTB, and energy-derived features from either Hamiltonian, gave no gain.

\section{Why 3D adds little: the geometries carry one number about the metal}
\label{sec:chem}

Eight different 3D encoders trained on the GFN2-xTB complexes were mutually interchangeable (effective rank 1.05 of their out-of-fold predictions).
That is a property of the input, not of the networks.
In GFN2-xTB every lanthanide-specific parameter is a linear interpolation in $Z$ between the Ce and Lu anchors, so along the series the optimised metal--donor shell can respond to the metal through exactly one scalar.
We tested how well that scalar is reproduced, and whether a Hamiltonian with explicit f electrons does better.

\paragraph{Benchmark.}
For 71 distinct extractants we take one relaxed complex each, substitute all 15 lanthanides at fixed topology, and re-optimise in the gas phase under three Hamiltonians with one binary and one protocol: GFN2-xTB (f in core), g-xTB~\cite{froitzheim2025gxtb} (f in valence, high-spin), and Ln-xTB~\cite{zhang2026lnxtb}, a 2026 re-fit of the 20 lanthanide parameters of GFN2-xTB element by element against all-electron bond lengths (parameters reconstructed from the supporting information and verified by reproducing all 35 published structure energies to $10^{-8}$\,$E_h$; Appendix~\ref{app:lnxtb}).
For each ligand and Hamiltonian the mean metal--donor distance is regressed on the Shannon radius across the 14 metals Ce--Lu; the slope $c_L$ is the ligand's \emph{contraction compliance}, with $c_L = 1$ meaning the complex contracts exactly as the ionic radii do.
All $3 \times 1{,}065$ optimisations converged.

\paragraph{Result.}
GFN2-xTB reproduces 40\,\% of the contraction, with a six-fold spread across ligands that is mostly noise (Table~\ref{tab:chem}, Figure~\ref{fig:contraction}); g-xTB reproduces it within 8\,\% on 70 of 71 ligands (paired $t = 43$).
Ln-xTB recovers the mean slope exactly (paired $t$ against g-xTB $= 0.1$), which confirms the linear-in-$Z$ diagnosis from a second direction: refitting the same functional form per element is enough to restore the slope.
But its series is jagged, with a $-0.10$\,\AA{} dip at Gd and $\pm 0.08$\,\AA{} swings at Ce--Nd that every ligand shares, a signature of independent per-element fits rather than chemistry.
Only the f-in-valence method is both right on average and regular.
For the learning problem the important number is the last column: under every Hamiltonian, most of the non-linear response to the metal is the same for all ligands.
A metal profile shared by all ligands is a function of metal identity, which the model already has from the metal descriptors; and the ligand-specific part, $c_L$, does not predict measured adjacent selectivity under any Hamiltonian (Pearson 0.11, $-0.02$ and 0.17 on 44 matched ligands).
So a better Hamiltonian fixes the chemistry without changing what a 3D model can learn, which is what the null result for g-xTB geometries in Table~\ref{tab:neg} says empirically.

\section{Limitations and outlook}

The binding constraint is data.
Seventy-six extractants contribute scored pairs, so each ligand's series is learned from about seventy-five others, and no feature engineering changes that count.
The attainable score is not identifiable from this dataset: separations reproduce to roughly 0.10--0.16 log units across independent condition sets, which bounds $R^2$ below about 0.65--0.85, but 70\,\% of scored pairs come from cells with no replicate, so a defensible ceiling needs designed replication.
Within one extractant, the model's series shape is weak (median per-extractant $R^2 = 0.09$); what it predicts well is which ligands are selective at all and where their large separations lie.
We therefore recommend reporting adjacent-pair models by ranking and enrichment with a bootstrap interval, keeping $R^2$ as a diagnostic, and spending effort on more ligands and on replication rather than on representations.

\section{Conclusion}

Adjacent-lanthanide selectivity can be learned from the literature record to a useful degree: $r = 0.59$ on unseen extractants, a two-fold enrichment of top-quartile ligands, and calibrated intervals.
The gain came from asking the model the right question, the shape of a series rather than its level, not from a better representation.
Three-dimensional structure helps only through that shape channel and only a little, and the reason is now measured rather than guessed: with the semiempirical methods in reach for thousands of complexes, a lanthanide complex geometry encodes one number about the metal, and even the method that gets the contraction right adds structure that every ligand shares.

\section{Protocol details}
\label{app:protocol}

\paragraph{Blocks.}
The ten binned condition descriptors that define a block are: acid class, acid strength, nitrate activity, pH, diluent family, modifier class, temperature, contact time, phase ratio and metal concentration.
Rows with identical extractant and bins form one block regardless of source publication.
Pairs with a measured $\log \mathrm{SF}$ of exactly zero, which arise when both metals sit at a detection limit, are excluded.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.}
CatBoost: 1{,}500 iterations, learning rate 0.04, depth 9, $\ell_2$ regularisation 3, feature subsampling (rsm) 0.3, quantile loss $\alpha = 0.6$; identical for the level and shape models.
3D encoder: width 96, three continuous-filter layers, 64 radial basis functions to 5\,\AA{}, cutoff 4\,\AA{}, heavy atoms only, dropout 0.15, head width 256, Adam with learning rate $2\times10^{-3}$ and weight decay $10^{-4}$, 60 epochs with early stopping on the adjacent-pair score of a validation fold, batches of 64 rows grouped by block so that the contrast term always has pairs.
Blend weight $w$: nested selection over $\{0, 0.05, \dots, 1\}$ with an equal-weight-per-extractant criterion; $w = 0.35$.

\paragraph{Confirmation set.}
The 444 pairs come from lanthanide rows with computed but uncurated geometries; their identities were written to the repository before any 3D model was trained on the expanded population, and they were scored once.

\section{Ln-xTB parameters}
\label{app:lnxtb}

The Ln-xTB parameters are given in the supporting information of~\citet{zhang2026lnxtb} as PDF tables only.
We parsed both copies (Tables S60--S61 and S62; no disagreement), replaced the $Z = 57$--$71$ blocks of the stock GFN2-xTB parameter file with them, and ran unmodified xtb 6.7.1.
Single points on the 35 optimised structures shipped with the paper reproduce the published energies to better than $2\times10^{-8}$\,$E_h$ once the paper's spin convention is used: the high-spin count of unpaired f electrons, reduced by one where its parity is inconsistent with the electron count (Ce 0, Pr 2, Nd 2, Pm 4, Sm 4, Eu 6, Gd 6, Tb 6, Dy 4, Ho 4, Er 2, Tm 2, Yb 0).
A closed-shell control gives $c_L = 0.981 \pm 0.140$ with the same jagged series.
